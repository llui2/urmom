\title{Adaptive response geometry}
\maketitle

A system has a finite set of possible responses $\{\mathbf a_i\}$ and adapts to an environment $\mathbf y$ by redistributing weight among them. The covariance
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf C
=
\sum_i
p_i
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
\end{equation*}
is not introduced as a separate measure of adaptability. It follows directly from the dynamics as
\begin{equation*}
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\mathbf C
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r),
\end{equation*}
so its eigenvectors and eigenvalues determine the directions and rates of local adaptation. Adaptation therefore changes both the present response and the operator governing the next response. When the environment selects only part of the available repertoire, the weights concentrate and some response directions are progressively suppressed. We derive that targets in the interior, on the boundary, and outside the attainable response set produce distinct asymptotic depletion laws and, after an environmental switch, distinct recovery times.

\section{Results}

The law of requisite variety relates regulation to the set of responses available to a system \cite{ashby1956}. Related ideas appear in response diversity \cite{elmqvist2003} and in multivariate evolutionary dynamics, where covariance constrains the directions of response to selection \cite{lande1979}. We consider the simplest setting in which the available responses are fixed and only their weights adapt. No additional diversity or adaptability functional is introduced: the attainable response set follows from the simplex constraint, while the response operator follows from the covariance generated by the same weights. This is sufficient to derive how adaptation to one environment changes the timescale of adaptation to the next.

\subsection{Adaptive response dynamics}

Let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf a_i\in\mathbb R^d,
\qquad
i=1,\ldots,n,
\end{equation}
be a finite set of response vectors. Their nonnegative weights satisfy
\begin{equation}
p_i\geq0,
\qquad
\sum_i p_i=1,
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
and the realized response is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r
=
\sum_i p_i\mathbf a_i.
\label{eq:response}
\end{equation}
We assume that the vectors $\mathbf a_i$ affinely span $\mathbb R^d$. If they span a lower-dimensional affine subspace, the same construction applies after restricting all response coordinates to that subspace.

Let $\mathbf y(t)\in\mathbb R^d$ denote the response required by the environment and define the mismatch
\begin{equation}
E
=
\frac12
\|\mathbf r-\mathbf y\|^2.
\label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
We let the weights follow the Shahshahani gradient flow of $E$ on the simplex \cite{shahshahani1979},
\begin{equation}
\dot p_i
=
-p_i
\left(
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_i}
-
\sum_j
p_j
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_j}
\right).
\label{eq:shahshahani}
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_i}
=
\mathbf a_i^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf r-\mathbf y),
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shahshahani} becomes
\begin{equation}
\dot p_i
=
p_i
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r).
\label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
The simplex is invariant under Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator}. If $p_i(0)>0$, then $p_i(t)>0$ for every finite $t$.

Define the weighted covariance of the response vectors,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C
=
\sum_i
p_i
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}.
\label{eq:covariance}
\end{equation}
Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:response} and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator} gives
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\mathbf C
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r).
\label{eq:response-dynamics}
\end{equation}
For a fixed target,
\begin{equation}
\dot E
=
-(\mathbf r-\mathbf y)^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C
(\mathbf r-\mathbf y)
\leq0.
\label{eq:error-decay}
\end{equation}
At a matched state, a small target perturbation $\delta\mathbf y$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\delta\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\mathbf C
(\delta\mathbf y-\delta\mathbf r).
\label{eq:linear-response}
\end{equation}
This relation holds to first order. The eigenvectors of $\mathbf C$ therefore give the local response directions, while its nonzero eigenvalues give the corresponding relaxation rates.

\subsection{Exponential coordinates}

Equation~\eqref{eq:replicator} can be integrated after introducing
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)
=
\int_0^t
\left[
\mathbf y(s)-\mathbf r(s)
\right]
\,\mathrm ds.
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
The weights then satisfy
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
=
\frac{
p_i(0)
e^{\mathbf a_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta(t)}
}{
\sum_j
p_j(0)
e^{\mathbf a_j^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta(t)}
}.
\label{eq:weights-solution}
\end{equation}
Introduce
\begin{equation}
\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)
=
\log
\sum_i
p_i(0)
e^{\mathbf a_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta}.
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
The first two derivatives of $\psi$ are
\begin{equation}
\nabla\psi
=
\mathbf r,
\qquad
\nabla^2\psi
=
\mathbf C.
\label{eq:gradient-hessian}
\end{equation}
These are the standard mean and covariance identities of an exponential family \cite{amari2016}. The dynamics reduce to
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
=
\mathbf y(t)
-
\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol\theta).
\label{eq:theta-dynamics}
\end{equation}
For constant $\mathbf y$, define
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathbf y}(\boldsymbol\theta)
=
\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)
-
\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta.
\label{eq:potential}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
=
-\nabla V_{\mathbf y},
\label{eq:gradient-flow}
\end{equation}
so the long-time behavior is determined by the geometry of $\psi$ relative to $\mathbf y$.

\subsection{Geometry of the available responses}

The set of all responses that can be obtained by redistributing the weights is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R
=
\operatorname{conv}
\{\mathbf a_1,\ldots,\mathbf a_n\}.
\label{eq:repertoire}
\end{equation}

\noindent\hrulefill

For points $\mathbf a_1,\ldots,\mathbf a_n$, their convex hull is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{conv}
\{\mathbf a_i\}
=
\left\{
\sum_i
q_i\mathbf a_i
:
q_i\geq0,
\ \sum_i q_i=1
\right\}.
\label{eq:convex-hull-definition}
\end{equation}
Hence $\mathcal R$ is exactly the set of responses compatible with Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex}. Since the $\mathbf a_i$ affinely span $\mathbb R^d$, $\mathcal R$ has a nonempty interior in $\mathbb R^d$.

A face $F$ of $\mathcal R$ is the subset on which a linear functional is maximal. Thus there exist a nonzero vector $\mathbf n$ and a scalar $h$ such that
\begin{equation}
F
=
\left\{
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R:
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=h
\right\},
\qquad
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\leq h
\quad
\text{for all }
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R.
\label{eq:face-definition}
\end{equation}
The hyperplane
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=h
\end{equation}
is a supporting hyperplane of $\mathcal R$. In two dimensions, a proper face is either an edge or a vertex.

\noindent\hrulefill

Figure~\ref{fig:response-geometry} summarizes the three possible positions of a constant target relative to the available response set.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1}
\caption{Geometry of the available response set. An interior target lies within the convex hull of the response vectors. A boundary target lies on a face $F$, with $g_i$ measuring the normal separation of a response from its supporting hyperplane. For an exterior target, the closest attainable response is the Euclidean projection $\mathbf r_*=\Pi_{\mathcal R}\mathbf y$.}
\label{fig:response-geometry}
\end{figure}

The position of a constant target $\mathbf y$ relative to $\mathcal R$ gives three different asymptotic regimes.

If
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y\in\operatorname{int}\mathcal R,
\end{equation}
there exists a finite $\boldsymbol\theta_*$ such that
\begin{equation}
\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol\theta_*)
=
\mathbf y.
\label{eq:interior-critical}
\end{equation}
Since the initial weights are positive and the $\mathbf a_i$ affinely span $\mathbb R^d$, the Hessian
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C_*
=
\nabla^2\psi(\boldsymbol\theta_*)
\end{equation}
is positive definite. Thus
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)\to\boldsymbol\theta_*,
\qquad
\mathbf r(t)\to\mathbf y,
\qquad
\mathbf C(t)\to\mathbf C_*.
\label{eq:interior-limit}
\end{equation}
All response directions retain a finite linear relaxation rate.

\subsection{Boundary targets}

Let $\mathbf y$ lie in the relative interior of a proper codimension-one face $F$ of $\mathcal R$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:face-definition}, choose a unit normal $\mathbf n$ such that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_i
=
h
\quad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\in F,
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
g_i
=
h-\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_i
>0
\quad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\notin F.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The quantity $g_i$ is the signed separation of $\mathbf a_i$ from the supporting hyperplane along $\mathbf n$. Let
\begin{equation}
g
=
\min_{\mathbf a_i\notin F}g_i.
\label{eq:min-gap}
\end{equation}

For any finite $\boldsymbol\theta$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} gives positive weight to every response. The corresponding mean $\mathbf r$ therefore lies in $\operatorname{int}\mathcal R$. Reaching a target on $F$ requires
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol\theta(t)\|
\to\infty.
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}
s(t)
=
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta(t).
\end{equation}
Since $\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y=h$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta-dynamics} gives
\begin{equation}
\dot s
=
h-\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf r
=
\sum_{\mathbf a_i\notin F}
p_i g_i.
\label{eq:normal-dynamics}
\end{equation}

Decompose
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta
=
s\mathbf n
+
\boldsymbol\theta_{\parallel},
\qquad
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta_{\parallel}=0.
\end{equation}
Because $\mathbf y$ lies in the relative interior of $F$, the exponential family restricted to the responses on $F$ has a finite tangential coordinate that realizes $\mathbf y$. The tangential component therefore approaches a finite limit while $s\to\infty$. Equation~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} then gives
\begin{equation}
p_i
\asymp
e^{-g_i s}
\qquad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\notin F.
\label{eq:boundary-weight-s}
\end{equation}
Here $f\asymp g$ means that $f/g$ remains bounded above and below by positive constants for sufficiently large time. The terms with the smallest gap $g$ dominate Eq.~\eqref{eq:normal-dynamics}, so
\begin{equation}
\dot s
\asymp
e^{-gs}.
\end{equation}
Integrating gives
\begin{equation}
s(t)
=
\frac{1}{g}\log t+O(1),
\label{eq:boundary-s}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
\asymp
t^{-g_i/g}.
\label{eq:boundary-weights}
\end{equation}
In particular, responses with $g_i=g$ decay as $t^{-1}$.

The same asymptotic law appears in the covariance along the normal direction. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:covariance},
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C
\mathbf n
=
\sum_i
p_i
\left[
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:normal-covariance}
\end{equation}
The points on $F$ share the same normal coordinate, while the closest points outside $F$ have nonzero gap $g$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C(t)
\mathbf n
\asymp
\frac{1}{t}.
\label{eq:boundary-capacity}
\end{equation}
The target is approached exactly, but the relaxation rate normal to the selected face vanishes algebraically.

Figure~\ref{fig:covariance-response} gives a geometric view of the same change in response capacity. When the weights are distributed across responses spanning $\mathcal R$, the covariance has finite extent in both directions. As adaptation concentrates weight on a face $F$, the local covariance geometry collapses toward directions tangent to that face. The normal response capacity is therefore progressively suppressed, consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:boundary-capacity}.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2}
\caption{Support concentration reshapes local response geometry. Marker size represents the weights $p_i$, while the gray shapes schematically indicate the local covariance geometry. With broad support, the response remains extended in both directions. After specialization toward a face $F$, the weights concentrate on that face and the covariance becomes nearly tangent to it, suppressing response capacity in the normal direction.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Targets outside the response set}

Now let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y\notin\mathcal R.
\end{equation}
Define the Euclidean projection of $\mathbf y$ onto $\mathcal R$ by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r_*
=
\Pi_{\mathcal R}\mathbf y
=
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf x\in\mathcal R}
\|\mathbf y-\mathbf x\|.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}

\noindent\hrulefill

For a closed convex set $\mathcal R$, the projection $\mathbf r_*$ is uniquely defined and satisfies
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r_*)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf x-\mathbf r_*)
\leq0
\qquad
\text{for every }
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R.
\label{eq:projection-condition}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e
=
\mathbf y-\mathbf r_*
\end{equation}
is normal to a supporting hyperplane of $\mathcal R$ at $\mathbf r_*$. This is the standard first-order characterization of Euclidean projection onto a convex set \cite{rockafellar1970}.

\noindent\hrulefill

Along the adaptive dynamics,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r(t)\to\mathbf r_*,
\end{equation}
and therefore Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta} gives
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)
=
\mathbf e\,t+o(t).
\label{eq:outside-theta}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
F_*
=
\left\{
\mathbf a_i:
\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_i
=
\max_j
\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_j
\right\}
\label{eq:exposed-face}
\end{equation}
be the face exposed by $\mathbf e$. For $\mathbf a_i\notin F_*$, define
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i
=
\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf r_*-\mathbf a_i)
>0.
\label{eq:external-gap}
\end{equation}
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:outside-theta} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} gives
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
=
e^{-\Delta_i t+o(t)}
\qquad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\notin F_*.
\label{eq:outside-weights}
\end{equation}
Thus responses away from the limiting face are suppressed exponentially when the target lies outside the available response set.

\subsection{Response after an environmental switch}

Consider a target $\mathbf y^-$ applied until time $T$, followed by a second target $\mathbf y^+$. Let response $i$ have weight $p_i(T)$ at the switching time. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator},
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}
\log p_i
=
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf y^+-\mathbf r).
\label{eq:log-growth}
\end{equation}
Assume that while $p_i$ grows from $p_i(T)$ to a fixed threshold $\eta$, its growth rate satisfies
\begin{equation}
0<\sigma_-
\leq
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf y^+-\mathbf r)
\leq
\sigma_+.
\label{eq:growth-bounds}
\end{equation}
If $\tau_i(T)$ denotes the first time after the switch at which $p_i=\eta$, integration of Eq.~\eqref{eq:log-growth} yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\sigma_+}
\log
\frac{\eta}{p_i(T)}
\leq
\tau_i(T)
\leq
\frac{1}{\sigma_-}
\log
\frac{\eta}{p_i(T)}.
\label{eq:recovery-bound}
\end{equation}

For an interior first target, $p_i(T)$ approaches a positive limit and the recovery time remains $O(1)$. For a boundary target, Eq.~\eqref{eq:boundary-weights} gives
\begin{equation}
p_i(T)
\asymp
T^{-\alpha_i},
\qquad
\alpha_i=\frac{g_i}{g},
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:recovery-bound} gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
\Theta(\log T).
\label{eq:boundary-recovery}
\end{equation}
For an unattainable first target, Eq.~\eqref{eq:outside-weights} gives
\begin{equation}
p_i(T)
=
e^{-\Delta_iT+o(T)},
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
\Theta(T).
\label{eq:outside-recovery}
\end{equation}

The duration of the first exposure therefore enters the later recovery time through the asymptotic depletion law of the required response. The recovery time is not an additional response metric: it is the first-passage time of the same adaptive dynamics after the target changes.

The model contains no mechanism for creating new vectors $\mathbf a_i$. Its conclusions concern redistribution within a fixed response set and the dependence of later relaxation on that redistribution.
