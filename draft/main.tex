\title{Phenotypic specialization creates history-dependent adaptation times}
\maketitle

A heterogeneous population can preserve phenotypic alternatives that become useful when the environment changes. Adaptation to the present environment, however, can itself deplete those alternatives. We study a minimal population model with a finite set of phenotypic states $\{\mathbf a_i\}$ occupied with fractions $p_i$. The population-mean phenotype
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf r
=
\sum_i p_i\mathbf a_i
\end{equation*}
adapts toward an environment-dependent target $\mathbf y$. The same redistribution changes the phenotypic covariance
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf C
=
\sum_i
p_i
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T},
\end{equation*}
which enters directly in the response law
\begin{equation*}
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\mathbf C
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r).
\end{equation*}
Exposure therefore changes both the current population phenotype and the directions in which the population can subsequently respond. We show that the geometry of the environmental target relative to the available phenotypic repertoire determines how alternatives are depleted. Interior targets preserve finite phenotypic variation, boundary targets suppress unused phenotypes algebraically, and unattainable targets suppress them exponentially. After an environmental switch, these regimes produce recovery times $O(1)$, $\Theta(\log T)$, and $\Theta(T)$ as a function of the duration $T$ of the first exposure.

\section{Results}

Populations in variable environments can respond by tracking environmental cues or by maintaining phenotypic heterogeneity, including bet-hedging strategies in which different phenotypes are retained because different environments favor them \cite{kussell2005,childs2010,xue2019}. Such heterogeneity can leave a subpopulation prepared for a future environmental change \cite{dejong2011,villamartin2019}. Bet hedging is a specific evolutionary strategy defined through long-term fitness under environmental uncertainty, and should not be identified with phenotypic heterogeneity in general \cite{dejong2011}. Here we ask a different dynamical question: how does adaptation to one environment change the composition of a heterogeneous population, and how does that altered composition constrain adaptation to the next environment?

We consider the simplest setting in which the available phenotypic states are fixed and only their population fractions change. The model does not optimize a strategy over many environments. Instead, it isolates the depletion caused by exposure to the current environment and the recovery cost that follows when the environment changes.

\subsection{Phenotypic composition dynamics}

Let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf a_i\in\mathbb R^d,
\qquad
i=1,\ldots,n,
\end{equation}
represent a finite set of phenotypic states. The coordinates of $\mathbf a_i$ may describe any collection of measurable traits relevant to the environmental response. Let $p_i$ be the fraction of the population in phenotype $i$, with
\begin{equation}
p_i\geq0,
\qquad
\sum_i p_i=1.
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
The population-mean phenotype is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r
=
\sum_i p_i\mathbf a_i.
\label{eq:response}
\end{equation}
We assume that the vectors $\mathbf a_i$ affinely span $\mathbb R^d$. If they span a lower-dimensional affine subspace, the same construction applies after restricting all phenotype coordinates to that subspace.

Let $\mathbf y(t)\in\mathbb R^d$ denote the phenotype favored by the current environment and define the population-level mismatch
\begin{equation}
E
=
\frac12
\|\mathbf r-\mathbf y\|^2.
\label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
We use the simplest frequency dynamics that decreases this mismatch while preserving the simplex: the Shahshahani gradient flow of $E$ \cite{shahshahani1979},
\begin{equation}
\dot p_i
=
-p_i
\left(
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_i}
-
\sum_j
p_j
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_j}
\right).
\label{eq:shahshahani}
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_i}
=
\mathbf a_i^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf r-\mathbf y),
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shahshahani} becomes
\begin{equation}
\dot p_i
=
p_i
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r).
\label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
This has the standard replicator form: phenotypes whose trait vector is better aligned with the current environmental mismatch increase in relative abundance. The model is therefore a phenomenological description of composition change under differential expansion or selection; it does not specify the intracellular mechanism that generates the phenotypic states. The simplex is invariant under Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator}. If $p_i(0)>0$, then $p_i(t)>0$ for every finite $t$.

Define the phenotypic covariance
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C
=
\sum_i
p_i
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}.
\label{eq:covariance}
\end{equation}
Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:response} and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator} gives
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\mathbf C
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r).
\label{eq:response-dynamics}
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathbf C$ is not introduced as an additional diversity or adaptability score. It is the covariance generated by the current population composition, and it appears directly as the local response operator. For a fixed environment,
\begin{equation}
\dot E
=
-(\mathbf r-\mathbf y)^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C
(\mathbf r-\mathbf y)
\leq0.
\label{eq:error-decay}
\end{equation}
At a matched state, a small environmental change $\delta\mathbf y$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\delta\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\mathbf C
(\delta\mathbf y-\delta\mathbf r).
\label{eq:linear-response}
\end{equation}
The eigenvectors of $\mathbf C$ therefore give the local response directions, while its nonzero eigenvalues give the corresponding relaxation rates. Changing the population composition changes these directions and rates.

\subsection{Exponential coordinates}

Equation~\eqref{eq:replicator} can be integrated after introducing
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)
=
\int_0^t
\left[
\mathbf y(s)-\mathbf r(s)
\right]
\,\mathrm ds.
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
The population fractions then satisfy
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
=
\frac{
p_i(0)
e^{\mathbf a_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta(t)}
}{
\sum_j
p_j(0)
e^{\mathbf a_j^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta(t)}
}.
\label{eq:weights-solution}
\end{equation}
Introduce
\begin{equation}
\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)
=
\log
\sum_i
p_i(0)
e^{\mathbf a_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta}.
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
The first two derivatives of $\psi$ are
\begin{equation}
\nabla\psi
=
\mathbf r,
\qquad
\nabla^2\psi
=
\mathbf C.
\label{eq:gradient-hessian}
\end{equation}
These are the standard mean and covariance identities of an exponential family \cite{amari2016}. The dynamics reduce to
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
=
\mathbf y(t)
-
\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol\theta).
\label{eq:theta-dynamics}
\end{equation}
For constant $\mathbf y$, define
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathbf y}(\boldsymbol\theta)
=
\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)
-
\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta.
\label{eq:potential}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
=
-\nabla V_{\mathbf y},
\label{eq:gradient-flow}
\end{equation}
so the long-time population composition is determined by the geometry of $\psi$ relative to the environmental target.

\subsection{Geometry of the phenotypic repertoire}

The set of all population-mean phenotypes that can be obtained by redistributing the fractions is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R
=
\operatorname{conv}
\{\mathbf a_1,\ldots,\mathbf a_n\}.
\label{eq:repertoire}
\end{equation}
We refer to $\mathcal R$ as the phenotypic repertoire of the population.

\noindent\hrulefill

For points $\mathbf a_1,\ldots,\mathbf a_n$, their convex hull is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{conv}
\{\mathbf a_i\}
=
\left\{
\sum_i
q_i\mathbf a_i
:
q_i\geq0,
\ \sum_i q_i=1
\right\}.
\label{eq:convex-hull-definition}
\end{equation}
Hence $\mathcal R$ is exactly the set of mean phenotypes compatible with Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex}. Since the $\mathbf a_i$ affinely span $\mathbb R^d$, $\mathcal R$ has a nonempty interior in $\mathbb R^d$.

A face $F$ of $\mathcal R$ is the subset on which a linear functional is maximal. Thus there exist a nonzero vector $\mathbf n$ and a scalar $h$ such that
\begin{equation}
F
=
\left\{
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R:
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=h
\right\},
\qquad
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\leq h
\quad
\text{for all }
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R.
\label{eq:face-definition}
\end{equation}
The hyperplane
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=h
\end{equation}
is a supporting hyperplane of $\mathcal R$. In two dimensions, a proper face is either an edge or a vertex.

\noindent\hrulefill

Figure~\ref{fig:response-geometry} summarizes the three possible positions of an environment-selected target relative to the available phenotypic repertoire.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1}
\caption{Geometry of the phenotypic repertoire. Each vertex represents an available phenotype $\mathbf a_i$, and $\mathbf r$ is the current population-mean phenotype. An interior environmental target can be realized by a mixture of available phenotypes. A boundary target lies on a face $F$, with $g_i$ measuring the normal separation of phenotype $i$ from its supporting hyperplane. For an unattainable target, the closest realizable population phenotype is the Euclidean projection $\mathbf r_*=\Pi_{\mathcal R}\mathbf y$.}
\label{fig:response-geometry}
\end{figure}

The position of a constant environmental target $\mathbf y$ relative to $\mathcal R$ gives three distinct asymptotic regimes.

If
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y\in\operatorname{int}\mathcal R,
\end{equation}
there exists a finite $\boldsymbol\theta_*$ such that
\begin{equation}
\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol\theta_*)
=
\mathbf y.
\label{eq:interior-critical}
\end{equation}
Since the initial fractions are positive and the $\mathbf a_i$ affinely span $\mathbb R^d$, the Hessian
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C_*
=
\nabla^2\psi(\boldsymbol\theta_*)
\end{equation}
is positive definite. Thus
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)\to\boldsymbol\theta_*,
\qquad
\mathbf r(t)\to\mathbf y,
\qquad
\mathbf C(t)\to\mathbf C_*.
\label{eq:interior-limit}
\end{equation}
All phenotypic states retain a positive limiting fraction, and all response directions retain a finite linear relaxation rate. Adaptation to an interior target therefore does not asymptotically eliminate any of the available alternatives.

\subsection{Boundary targets and phenotypic specialization}

Let $\mathbf y$ lie in the relative interior of a proper codimension-one face $F$ of $\mathcal R$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:face-definition}, choose a unit normal $\mathbf n$ such that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_i
=
h
\quad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\in F,
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
g_i
=
h-\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_i
>0
\quad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\notin F.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The quantity $g_i$ is the separation of phenotype $i$ from the selected face along $\mathbf n$. Let
\begin{equation}
g
=
\min_{\mathbf a_i\notin F}g_i.
\label{eq:min-gap}
\end{equation}

For any finite $\boldsymbol\theta$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} gives positive abundance to every phenotype. The corresponding mean $\mathbf r$ therefore lies in $\operatorname{int}\mathcal R$. Reaching a target on $F$ requires
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol\theta(t)\|
\to\infty.
\end{equation}
Write
\begin{equation}
s(t)
=
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta(t).
\end{equation}
Since $\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y=h$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta-dynamics} gives
\begin{equation}
\dot s
=
h-\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\mathbf r
=
\sum_{\mathbf a_i\notin F}
p_i g_i.
\label{eq:normal-dynamics}
\end{equation}

Decompose
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta
=
s\mathbf n
+
\boldsymbol\theta_{\parallel},
\qquad
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\theta_{\parallel}=0.
\end{equation}
Because $\mathbf y$ lies in the relative interior of $F$, the exponential family restricted to the phenotypes on $F$ has a finite tangential coordinate that realizes $\mathbf y$. The tangential component therefore approaches a finite limit while $s\to\infty$. Equation~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} then gives
\begin{equation}
p_i
\asymp
e^{-g_i s}
\qquad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\notin F.
\label{eq:boundary-weight-s}
\end{equation}
Here $f\asymp g$ means that $f/g$ remains bounded above and below by positive constants for sufficiently large time. The terms with the smallest gap $g$ dominate Eq.~\eqref{eq:normal-dynamics}, so
\begin{equation}
\dot s
\asymp
e^{-gs}.
\end{equation}
Integrating gives
\begin{equation}
s(t)
=
\frac{1}{g}\log t+O(1),
\label{eq:boundary-s}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
\asymp
t^{-g_i/g}.
\label{eq:boundary-weights}
\end{equation}
In particular, the closest phenotypes outside the selected face decay as $t^{-1}$.

The same asymptotic law appears in the phenotypic covariance along the normal direction. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:covariance},
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C
\mathbf n
=
\sum_i
p_i
\left[
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:normal-covariance}
\end{equation}
The phenotypes on $F$ share the same normal coordinate, while those outside $F$ have a nonzero gap. Hence
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C(t)
\mathbf n
\asymp
\frac{1}{t}.
\label{eq:boundary-capacity}
\end{equation}
The population reaches the environmental target, but it does so by specializing onto the phenotypic face $F$. Variation normal to that face is progressively lost, and the corresponding response rate vanishes algebraically.

\subsection{Targets outside the phenotypic repertoire}

Now let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf y\notin\mathcal R.
\end{equation}
The environment favors a phenotype that cannot be realized by any mixture of the available states. Define the Euclidean projection of $\mathbf y$ onto $\mathcal R$ by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r_*
=
\Pi_{\mathcal R}\mathbf y
=
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf x\in\mathcal R}
\|\mathbf y-\mathbf x\|.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}

\noindent\hrulefill

For a closed convex set $\mathcal R$, the projection $\mathbf r_*$ is uniquely defined and satisfies
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf y-\mathbf r_*)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf x-\mathbf r_*)
\leq0
\qquad
\text{for every }
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R.
\label{eq:projection-condition}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e
=
\mathbf y-\mathbf r_*
\end{equation}
is normal to a supporting hyperplane of $\mathcal R$ at $\mathbf r_*$. This is the standard first-order characterization of Euclidean projection onto a convex set \cite{rockafellar1970}.

\noindent\hrulefill

Along the adaptive dynamics,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r(t)\to\mathbf r_*,
\end{equation}
and therefore Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta} gives
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)
=
\mathbf e\,t+o(t).
\label{eq:outside-theta}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
F_*
=
\left\{
\mathbf a_i:
\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_i
=
\max_j
\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a_j
\right\}
\label{eq:exposed-face}
\end{equation}
be the phenotypic face exposed by $\mathbf e$. For $\mathbf a_i\notin F_*$, define
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i
=
\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf r_*-\mathbf a_i)
>0.
\label{eq:external-gap}
\end{equation}
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:outside-theta} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} gives
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
=
e^{-\Delta_i t+o(t)}
\qquad
\text{for }
\mathbf a_i\notin F_*.
\label{eq:outside-weights}
\end{equation}
Thus an unattainable environmental target drives exponential depletion of phenotypes away from the limiting face. This is a stronger form of specialization than for a boundary target.

\subsection{Adaptation after an environmental change}

Consider an environment with target $\mathbf y^-$ applied until time $T$, followed by a second environment with target $\mathbf y^+$. Suppose phenotype $i$ has been depleted during the first exposure and becomes advantageous after the switch. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator},
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}
\log p_i
=
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf y^+-\mathbf r).
\label{eq:log-growth}
\end{equation}
Assume that while $p_i$ grows from $p_i(T)$ to a fixed observable threshold $\eta$, its growth rate satisfies
\begin{equation}
0<\sigma_-
\leq
(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf r)^{\mathsf T}
(\mathbf y^+-\mathbf r)
\leq
\sigma_+.
\label{eq:growth-bounds}
\end{equation}
If $\tau_i(T)$ denotes the first time after the environmental switch at which $p_i=\eta$, integration of Eq.~\eqref{eq:log-growth} yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\sigma_+}
\log
\frac{\eta}{p_i(T)}
\leq
\tau_i(T)
\leq
\frac{1}{\sigma_-}
\log
\frac{\eta}{p_i(T)}.
\label{eq:recovery-bound}
\end{equation}

For an interior first target, $p_i(T)$ approaches a positive limit and the recovery time remains
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
O(1).
\end{equation}
For a boundary target, Eq.~\eqref{eq:boundary-weights} gives
\begin{equation}
p_i(T)
\asymp
T^{-\alpha_i},
\qquad
\alpha_i
=
\frac{g_i}{g},
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:recovery-bound} gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
\Theta(\log T).
\label{eq:boundary-recovery}
\end{equation}
For an unattainable first target, Eq.~\eqref{eq:outside-weights} gives
\begin{equation}
p_i(T)
=
e^{-\Delta_iT+o(T)},
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
\Theta(T).
\label{eq:outside-recovery}
\end{equation}

The duration of the first exposure therefore enters the later adaptation time through the abundance of phenotypic alternatives that survive that exposure. An environment that can be matched while retaining a broad mixture leaves no asymptotically growing memory in the recovery time. An environment that selects a boundary face creates a logarithmically growing lag, while sustained selection toward an unattainable phenotype creates a lag proportional to the duration of the previous exposure.

\section{Discussion}

Phenotypic heterogeneity is often discussed as a way for populations to cope with variable or uncertain environments \cite{kussell2005,childs2010,villamartin2019}. The mechanism studied here is complementary but distinct from bet hedging. We do not ask which distribution of phenotypes maximizes long-term population growth, and we do not introduce stochastic switching to maintain diversity. Instead, we ask how adaptation to the current environment changes the abundance of existing phenotypic alternatives. The resulting composition is itself a memory of past exposure because it determines how rapidly the population can respond when conditions change.

The model gives a direct experimental prediction. A heterogeneous population can be exposed to a first environment for different durations $T$, then transferred to a second environment that favors a phenotype depleted during the first exposure. Measuring both the depleted fraction $p_i(T)$ and the time required for that phenotype to return to a fixed abundance would test the relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:recovery-bound}. The theory predicts qualitatively different dependence on the first exposure time: bounded, logarithmic, or linear recovery according to whether the first environmental target lies in the interior, on the boundary, or outside the phenotypic repertoire.

The present model deliberately keeps the phenotypic states fixed. It contains no mutation, spontaneous phenotype switching, or creation of new states, all of which can replenish depleted subpopulations in real biological systems. Its purpose is to isolate the geometric consequence of composition change alone. In that setting, adaptation to one environment can progressively remove the phenotypic variation required for the next, producing history-dependent adaptation times without introducing an additional memory variable.
