\title{Frequency-dependent fitness on genotype space}
\maketitle

Fitness landscapes assign reproductive values to genotypes and thereby constrain which mutational paths are favored by selection \cite{weinreich2006,devisser2014}. Mutation and frequency-dependent selection have also been combined in replicator--mutator models \cite{page2002}. Recent experiments with bacteriophage $\lambda$ directly show that competitive context can change mutational effects and epistasis, deforming the measured fitness landscape during adaptation \cite{doud2024}. We consider a minimal discrete model in which mutation follows an explicit genotype graph while the current population changes relative fitness. The aim is to isolate how this feedback changes evolutionary accessibility.

\section{Model}

Consider binary genotypes $x_i \in \{0,1\}^L$, with frequencies $p_i(t) \geq 0$ and $\sum_i p_i(t) = 1$. Mutation connects genotypes at Hamming distance one. We define $Q_{ji}$ as the probability that genotype $j$ produces genotype $i$ after mutation,
\begin{equation}
Q_{ji}
=
\begin{cases}
1 - \mu, & i = j,\\[2pt]
\mu/L, & d_H(x_i,x_j) = 1,\\[2pt]
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:mutation-matrix}
\end{equation}

Each genotype has an intrinsic fitness $g_i$. Its effective fitness at generation $t$ is
\begin{equation}
f_i(t)
=
g_i
+
\beta \sum_j A_{ij} p_j(t),
\label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}$ measures how the frequency of genotype $j$ changes the fitness of genotype $i$. We therefore refer to $A$ as a fitness-coupling matrix.

Selection and mutation are applied in one discrete update. Define the selection normalization
\begin{equation}
Z(t)
=
\sum_k p_k(t) \exp[\eta f_k(t)].
\end{equation}
The population then evolves according to
\begin{equation}
p_i(t+1)
=
\sum_j
Q_{ji}
\frac{
p_j(t) \exp[\eta f_j(t)]
}{
Z(t)
}.
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
The vector $g$ defines the intrinsic landscape, $Q$ defines the mutation geometry, and $A$ changes relative fitness as the population changes.

When random landscapes are used, $g$ is drawn once per realization and kept fixed during Eq.~\eqref{eq:dynamics}. The original exploratory script used independent standard-normal entries and drew a new landscape between optimization runs, not during an evolutionary trajectory. The landscape is therefore quenched on the dynamical timescale. More generally, the distribution of $g$ can be chosen to control landscape correlations or ruggedness.

\section{Relative fitness and local accessibility}

Only fitness differences enter the selection step. For two genotypes $i$ and $k$,
\begin{equation}
f_i(t) - f_k(t)
=
g_i - g_k
+
\beta \sum_j
(A_{ij} - A_{kj}) p_j(t).
\label{eq:relative-fitness}
\end{equation}
The first term is fixed by the intrinsic landscape, while the second depends on population composition.

Consider a population concentrated at genotype $i$ and a rare one-step mutant $j$. During selection, the mutant-to-resident ratio is multiplied by
\begin{equation}
\exp\left[
\eta
\left(
g_j - g_i
+
\beta (A_{ji} - A_{ii})
\right)
\right].
\end{equation}
The mutant therefore increases relative to the resident when
\begin{equation}
g_j - g_i
+
\beta (A_{ji} - A_{ii})
>
0.
\label{eq:invasion}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:invasion} gives a local accessibility condition in the rare-mutant limit. Mutation creates the mutant through $Q$, while fitness coupling changes its selective effect after it appears. A step with $g_j < g_i$ can therefore become favorable in the population created by genotype $i$.

The matrix $A$ contains a redundant component. Replacing
\begin{equation}
A
\longrightarrow
A + \mathbf{1} v^{\mathsf T}
\end{equation}
adds the same quantity $v^{\mathsf T}p$ to every genotype fitness and leaves Eq.~\eqref{eq:relative-fitness} unchanged. The dynamics therefore depends on differences between rows of $A$, not on all matrix entries independently.

\section{Illustrative dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics} shows one fixed three-bit landscape. Genotype $000$ is an intrinsic local peak, genotype $111$ is the global peak, and the coupling acts only between Hamming-distance-one genotypes. With $\beta = 0$, the intrinsically unfavorable intermediate $001$ remains at mutation-scale frequency. With $\beta = 0.4$, the initial condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:invasion} favors $001$, which reaches finite frequency and changes the subsequent population trajectory. The figure is an illustration of the mechanism rather than a statistical statement about random landscapes.

\section{Minimal problem}

We first consider a rugged intrinsic landscape with a local peak and a higher peak elsewhere. Starting near the local peak, the baseline dynamics with $\beta = 0$ defines the mutational routes favored by the intrinsic landscape. We then introduce a low-dimensional family of fitness couplings and determine when Eq.~\eqref{eq:invasion} changes those routes.

A simple first family is
\begin{equation}
A_{ij}
=
a_{d_H(x_i,x_j)},
\label{eq:radial-coupling}
\end{equation}
where the coupling depends only on genetic distance. This keeps mutation and fitness coupling on the same genotype geometry and reduces the number of free coupling parameters from $O(4^L)$ to $O(L)$.

For a monomorphic resident $i$ and a one-step mutant $j$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:invasion} becomes
\begin{equation}
g_j - g_i
+
\beta (a_1 - a_0)
>
0.
\end{equation}
If $a_1 > a_0$, an intrinsically unfavorable edge becomes locally favorable above
\begin{equation}
\beta_{i\to j}
=
\frac{g_i-g_j}{a_1-a_0}.
\label{eq:edge-threshold}
\end{equation}
The first quantities to measure are the set of locally favorable mutational edges, escape from intrinsic local peaks, the resulting population trajectory, and their dependence on $\beta$, landscape ruggedness, and the coefficients $a_d$. Diversity is treated as an outcome of the dynamics rather than an optimization objective.

\section{Mathematical direction}

For the distance-dependent family in Eq.~\eqref{eq:radial-coupling}, both mutation and fitness coupling respect the symmetries of the hypercube and can be represented in the Walsh basis. This gives a complementary description in which mutation and frequency-dependent fitness act on the same genotype-space modes.

The local condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:invasion} and the spectral representation address different scales of the same dynamics. The first describes how population feedback changes individual mutational steps near a resident state. The second can be used to determine how the same feedback changes relaxation or amplification of larger-scale variation across genotype space.
