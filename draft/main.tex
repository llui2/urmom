\title{Frequency-dependent accessibility and correlated percolation on genotype space}
\maketitle

\section{Literature}

Fitness landscapes assign a reproductive value to each genotype and orient mutational steps toward higher fitness. In the strong-selection, weak-mutation limit this leads to adaptive walks on a genotype graph, and the existence and number of fitness-increasing paths quantify evolutionary accessibility \cite{weinreich2006,devisser2014,franke2012,zagorski2016}. Percolation ideas have entered this setting in two different ways. Holey-landscape models study connected clusters of viable genotypes \cite{gavrilets1997}, while accessibility percolation studies the appearance of monotonic fitness-increasing paths on random landscapes \cite{schmiegelt2023}. Standard bond percolation on the hypercube provides a third reference point: when edges are independently occupied, a giant connected component appears at an occupation probability asymptotic to $1/L$ \cite{vanderhofstad2017}. These constructions all use a connectivity rule that is fixed independently of the evolving population.

Frequency-dependent selection removes the corresponding fixed ordering of mutational steps. Replicator--mutator models already allow genotype growth rates to depend on population composition \cite{page2002}, and recent experiments show that this dependence can be strong. In bacteriophage $\lambda$, competition changes mutational effects and epistasis across the measured landscape, opening evolutionary routes that are absent in a static description \cite{doud2024}. Measurements in \emph{Escherichia coli} likewise find widespread frequency-dependent fitness effects and violations of a single transitive fitness ranking \cite{ascensao2025}. In ecology, invasion growth rates and invasion graphs are used to characterize conditions for persistence and coexistence \cite{hofbauer2022}. These results motivate a genotype-space problem in which mutational accessibility is itself generated by resident-dependent invasion.

We consider the simplest version of this problem. A static landscape gives an acyclic orientation of genotype space, whereas frequency-dependent feedback progressively makes selected mutational edges accessible in both resident backgrounds. The collective question is whether these locally opened edges join into a macroscopic mutually traversable region. This produces a percolation problem whose bonds are generated from the fitness landscape itself. The resulting edge variables are locally dependent because incident edges share a genotype fitness, separating the construction from ordinary independent bond percolation.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Local invasion defines the accessibility graph}

Consider binary genotypes $x_i \in \{0,1\}^L$ with frequencies $p_i \geq 0$ and $\sum_i p_i = 1$. Mutations connect genotypes at Hamming distance one. Each genotype has intrinsic fitness $g_i$, while the current population changes its effective fitness according to
\begin{equation}
f_i(p)
=
g_i
+
\beta \sum_k A_{ik} p_k,
\label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
where $A_{ik}$ measures the effect of genotype $k$ on genotype $i$ and $\beta \geq 0$ controls the strength of the feedback.

Let genotype $i$ be resident and let $j$ be a rare one-step mutant. At $p_i=1$, the mutant increases relative to the resident when
\begin{equation}
s_{ij}
=
g_j-g_i
+
\beta (A_{ji}-A_{ii})
>
0.
\label{eq:invasion}
\end{equation}
We therefore orient the mutational edge $i\to j$ whenever $s_{ij}>0$. Unlike a static fitness graph, this invasion graph combines comparisons made in different resident states.

We first take the coupling to depend only on genetic distance,
\begin{equation}
A_{ij}
=
a_{d_H(x_i,x_j)}.
\label{eq:radial-coupling}
\end{equation}
For neighboring genotypes, define $\Delta a=a_1-a_0$ and assume $\Delta a>0$. Equation~\eqref{eq:invasion} becomes
\begin{equation}
s_{ij}
=
g_j-g_i+\beta \Delta a.
\end{equation}
The direction toward larger intrinsic fitness is always accessible. The opposite direction becomes accessible when
\begin{equation}
\beta
>
\beta_{ij}
=
\frac{|g_i-g_j|}{\Delta a}.
\label{eq:edge-threshold}
\end{equation}
Thus every mutational edge has a well-defined opening threshold. At $\beta=0$, and in the absence of fitness ties, the invasion graph reduces to the acyclic orientation generated by $g$. Increasing $\beta$ does not replace this orientation; it progressively adds the reverse directions.

Let $R_\beta$ be the undirected subgraph containing only edges that are accessible in both resident backgrounds. Each connected component of $R_\beta$ is therefore a region that can be traversed in either direction through successive one-step invasions. The components of $R_\beta$ are contained in strongly connected components of the full invasion graph, but the two constructions need not coincide. We use $R_\beta$ because Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-threshold} turns its growth into an explicit threshold-percolation problem.

\subsection{Population dynamics realizes the local opening mechanism}

To connect the invasion condition to a population trajectory, define $Q_{ji}$ as the probability that genotype $j$ produces genotype $i$ after mutation,
\begin{equation}
Q_{ji}
=
\begin{cases}
1-\mu, & i=j,\\[2pt]
\mu/L, & d_H(x_i,x_j)=1,\\[2pt]
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Selection and mutation are applied in one discrete update,
\begin{equation}
p_i(t+1)
=
\sum_j
Q_{ji}
\frac{
p_j(t)\exp[\eta f_j(p(t))]
}{
\sum_k p_k(t)\exp[\eta f_k(p(t))]
}.
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics} uses a three-bit landscape with $\Delta a=1$. Genotype $000$ is an intrinsic local peak and $111$ is the global peak. With $\beta=0$, the intermediate $001$ remains at mutation-scale frequency. With $\beta=0.4$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-threshold} opens the step $000\to001$, which reaches finite frequency and changes the subsequent population trajectory. This example only illustrates the local mechanism; the collective transition is studied below over random landscapes.

\subsection{A minimal connectivity transition}

Let $C(\beta)$ denote the size of the largest connected component of $R_\beta$ and define
\begin{equation}
S(\beta)
=
\frac{C(\beta)}{2^L}.
\label{eq:order-parameter}
\end{equation}
A collective change can already occur on the three-dimensional cube. Figure~\ref{fig:small-transition} uses a schematic landscape in which the reversible graph consists of two four-genotype components below $\beta_c$. At $\beta_c$, one additional bridge edge satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-threshold} and joins the two components. The local opening of one edge therefore produces the global jump $S:1/2\to1$.

The bridge opening at $\beta_c=0.45$ produces the jump from $S=1/2$ to $S=1$.}

\subsection{Scaling to random genotype spaces}

The edge thresholds in Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-threshold} define a percolation-like ensemble once the intrinsic landscape is random. We take independent Gaussian fitness values $g_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and absorb $\Delta a$ into $\beta$. For a neighboring pair, $g_i-g_j\sim\mathcal N(0,2)$, so the probability that an edge belongs to $R_\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
q(\beta)
=
P(|g_i-g_j|<\beta)
=
\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right).
\label{eq:edge-probability}
\end{equation}
The hypercube has degree $L$, giving the mean reversible degree
\begin{equation}
c(\beta)
=
L q(\beta).
\label{eq:mean-degree}
\end{equation}
The marginal occupation probability in Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-probability} does not determine the connectivity transition because the edge states are not independent. Two incident edges share the fitness of their common genotype and are therefore correlated, whereas vertex-disjoint edges are independent. The random landscape thus generates a locally dependent bond process on the hypercube. For independent bond percolation, the large-$L$ giant-component threshold occurs near $c=1$ \cite{vanderhofstad2017}. Here $c=1$ is only a reference value; the correlations can modify both the threshold and the finite-size scaling.

Figure~\ref{fig:percolation} scales the construction to random landscapes with $L=6,8,10,12$. For each realization, edges are added in the order set by $\beta_{ij}$ and $S$ is measured as the threshold is increased. The ensemble-averaged component fraction develops a progressively sharper rise. The probability $P(S>1/2)$ also sharpens with increasing $L$, with its midpoint lying near $c\simeq1.5$ over the dimensions shown. This midpoint is not an estimate of the percolation threshold: the condition $S>1/2$ probes a macroscopically occupied component already beyond its first emergence. Locating the critical point requires observables such as the two largest components, susceptibility, and finite-size scaling across larger $L$.

\subsection{Connectivity as a structural condition for diversity}

The connectivity transition is not by itself a statement about coexistence. A large component of $R_\beta$ means that many genotypes are linked by chains of pairwise mutual invasibility, but it does not imply that those genotypes coexist simultaneously under Eq.~\eqref{eq:dynamics}. This distinction is central in coexistence theory, where persistence is determined from invasion growth rates of the full dynamical system rather than connectivity alone \cite{hofbauer2022}.

The percolation construction instead identifies a structural change in the evolutionary possibilities available to the population. Below the transition, mutually traversable regions occupy a small fraction of genotype space. Above it, a macroscopic region becomes connected by resident-dependent mutational steps. This suggests a concrete dynamical hypothesis: the emergence of the giant reversible component should coincide with a qualitative change in the diversity that can be maintained by the mutation--selection dynamics. A natural quantity to test is the effective genotype diversity
\begin{equation}
D(t)
=
\exp\left[
-\sum_i p_i(t)\log p_i(t)
\right],
\label{eq:diversity}
\end{equation}
together with its long-time or stationary value. The relevant comparison is then between the independently measured percolation observables of $R_\beta$ and the diversity generated by Eq.~\eqref{eq:dynamics}.

The current result therefore separates three levels of the problem. Equation~\eqref{eq:edge-threshold} gives the local frequency-dependent opening rule exactly. Figures~\ref{fig:small-transition} and~\ref{fig:percolation} show that these local openings can produce a collective connectivity transition. Whether that transition predicts persistent evolutionary diversity is a further dynamical question rather than an assumption of the percolation construction.
