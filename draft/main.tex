\title{History-dependent recovery in microbial resource competition}
\maketitle

Microbial strains competing for substitutable resources can coexist by dividing metabolic effort across different resource-use strategies. In a broad class of consumer--resource models, coexistence is controlled geometrically: the environmental resource supply must lie in the convex hull of the strategies present in the community \cite{macarthur1970,posfai2017,taillefumier2017,good2018}. We ask a dynamical question that is distinct from this equilibrium condition. If a community is exposed to one resource environment for a long time, how strongly are alternative strategies depleted, and how long does it take to recover when the resource supply changes?

We consider fixed resource strategies $\boldsymbol\alpha_i$ with strain frequencies $p_i$ and normalized resource supply $\boldsymbol\beta$. The community-mean strategy is
\begin{equation*}
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
=
\sum_i p_i \boldsymbol\alpha_i.
\end{equation*}
In the neutral fixed-strategy limit of substitutable-resource competition, the deterministic frequency dynamics can be written
\begin{equation*}
\dot p_i
=
p_i
\left[
\boldsymbol\alpha_i^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta \oslash \bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
-1
\right],
\end{equation*}
where $\oslash$ denotes componentwise division. This dynamics drives the community mean toward the resource supply while simultaneously changing the abundance of the strategies that make future responses possible. We show that the position of $\boldsymbol\beta$ relative to the convex hull of the available strategies gives three asymptotic regimes. Interior supplies preserve finite strain abundances, boundary supplies deplete off-face strains algebraically, and supplies outside the hull deplete them exponentially. After a switch in resource supply, these regimes produce recovery times $O(1)$, $\Theta(\log T)$, and $\Theta(T)$ as a function of the duration $T$ of the first exposure.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Resource competition dynamics}

Consider $d$ substitutable resources. Strain $i$ is characterized by a normalized resource-use strategy
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\alpha_i
\in
\mathbb R^d,
\qquad
\alpha_{ik} > 0,
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^d \alpha_{ik} = 1.
\label{eq:strategy}
\end{equation}
The environment supplies the resources in proportions
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\beta
\in
\mathbb R^d,
\qquad
\beta_k > 0,
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^d \beta_k = 1.
\label{eq:supply}
\end{equation}
The strain frequencies satisfy
\begin{equation}
p_i \geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_i p_i = 1,
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
and the community-mean resource strategy is
\begin{equation}
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
=
\sum_i p_i \boldsymbol\alpha_i.
\label{eq:mean-strategy}
\end{equation}

Consumer--resource models with a finite metabolic budget naturally lead to competition between such normalized strategies \cite{posfai2017,taillefumier2017}. In the fixed-strategy, equal-baseline-fitness limit used by Good, Martis, and Hallatschek, selection tunes the population-averaged uptake toward the environmental supply \cite{good2018}. We take the corresponding deterministic frequency dynamics
\begin{equation}
\dot p_i
=
p_i
\left[
\sum_{k=1}^d
\alpha_{ik}
\frac{\beta_k}{\bar\alpha_k}
-1
\right].
\label{eq:resource-replicator}
\end{equation}
The simplex is invariant because
\begin{equation}
\sum_i p_i
\sum_k
\alpha_{ik}
\frac{\beta_k}{\bar\alpha_k}
=
\sum_k \beta_k
=
1.
\end{equation}
Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-replicator} changes only the relative abundance of the competing strategies.

The dynamics has a direct variational interpretation. Define the mismatch between the supplied and population-averaged resource compositions by
\begin{equation}
E
=
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
\middle\|
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
=
\sum_k
\beta_k
\log
\frac{\beta_k}{\bar\alpha_k}.
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial E}{\partial p_i}
=
-
\sum_k
\alpha_{ik}
\frac{\beta_k}{\bar\alpha_k},
\end{equation}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:resource-replicator} is the Shahshahani gradient flow of $E$ on the frequency simplex \cite{shahshahani1979}.

Define the covariance of resource strategies
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C
=
\sum_i
p_i
\left(
\boldsymbol\alpha_i-
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
\left(
\boldsymbol\alpha_i-
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)^{\mathsf T}.
\label{eq:covariance}
\end{equation}
Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:mean-strategy} gives
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}}
=
\mathbf C
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right).
\end{equation}
Because all strategy vectors are normalized,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C \mathbf 1 = 0,
\end{equation}
so the same equation can be written as
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}}
=
\mathbf C
\mathbf D
\left(
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)^{-1}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
-
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right),
\label{eq:mean-dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf D(\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha})$ is the diagonal matrix with entries $\bar\alpha_k$. The covariance therefore appears directly in the ecological response law, but with the resource-dependent metric inherited from the consumer--resource dynamics.

For fixed $\boldsymbol\beta$,
\begin{equation}
\dot E
=
-
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
\leq 0.
\label{eq:kl-decay}
\end{equation}
Near a matched state $\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}=\boldsymbol\beta$, the KL divergence has the quadratic expansion
\begin{equation}
E
=
\frac12
\left(
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}-\boldsymbol\beta
\right)^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf D(\boldsymbol\beta)^{-1}
\left(
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}-\boldsymbol\beta
\right)
+
O
\left(
\|\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}-\boldsymbol\beta\|^3
\right).
\label{eq:local-kl}
\end{equation}
Thus the quadratic response model arises locally from the consumer--resource mismatch rather than being imposed as a biological fitness function.

\subsection{Geometry of the available resource strategies}

The set of community-mean strategies attainable by changing strain frequencies is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R
=
\operatorname{conv}
\left\{
\boldsymbol\alpha_1,
\ldots,
\boldsymbol\alpha_n
\right\}.
\label{eq:repertoire}
\end{equation}
This convex-hull construction is already central to resource-competition models: when the normalized resource supply lies inside the hull of the available consumption strategies, coexistence can tune the community-average uptake to match the supply \cite{posfai2017,good2018}. Related geometric formulations have been developed for coexistence and perturbation response in more general consumer--resource systems \cite{blumenthal2023,goyal2025}.

Figure~\ref{fig:response-geometry} summarizes the three positions of the resource supply relative to the strategy hull.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1}
\caption{Geometry of resource competition. Each point $\boldsymbol\alpha_i$ is a strain resource-use strategy, $\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}$ is the current community mean, and $\boldsymbol\beta$ is the normalized resource supply. An interior supply can be matched by a mixture with broad support. A boundary supply lies on a face $F$, with $g_i$ measuring the separation of strategy $i$ from the supporting hyperplane. When the supply lies outside $\mathcal R$, the community converges to the KL projection $\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}_*$ of $\boldsymbol\beta$ onto the attainable strategy set.}
\label{fig:response-geometry}
\end{figure}

To analyze depletion of rare strains, introduce dual coordinates
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
=
\boldsymbol\beta
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
-
\mathbf 1.
\label{eq:theta-dynamics}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:resource-replicator} then integrates to
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
=
\frac{
p_i(0)
\exp
\left[
\boldsymbol\alpha_i^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)
\right]
}{
\sum_j
p_j(0)
\exp
\left[
\boldsymbol\alpha_j^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol\theta(t)
\right]
}.
\label{eq:weights-solution}
\end{equation}
This form makes the asymptotic distinction between interior, boundary, and exterior supplies explicit.

\subsection{Interior resource supply}

Suppose
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\beta
\in
\operatorname{relint}
\mathcal R.
\end{equation}
Then there is a finite dual coordinate $\boldsymbol\theta_*$ for which
\begin{equation}
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\left(
\boldsymbol\theta_*
\right)
=
\boldsymbol\beta.
\end{equation}
Starting from positive frequencies, Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights-solution} therefore converges to a finite composition with
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
\to
p_i^* > 0.
\label{eq:interior-frequencies}
\end{equation}
No strain is asymptotically depleted. The resource supply is matched without driving the community onto a lower-dimensional face of its strategy hull.

\subsection{Boundary supply and algebraic depletion}

Now let $\boldsymbol\beta$ lie in the relative interior of a proper codimension-one face $F$ of $\mathcal R$. Choose a normal $\mathbf n$ and scalar $h$ such that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol\alpha_i
=
h
\qquad
\text{for }
\boldsymbol\alpha_i \in F,
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
g_i
=
h
-
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol\alpha_i
>
0
\qquad
\text{for }
\boldsymbol\alpha_i \notin F.
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
g
=
\min_{\boldsymbol\alpha_i\notin F}
g_i.
\end{equation}

Matching a boundary supply requires the frequencies of strains outside $F$ to vanish. The dual coordinate therefore diverges in the exposed normal direction. Writing
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\theta
=
s\mathbf n
+
\boldsymbol\theta_{\parallel},
\end{equation}
with bounded tangential component gives from Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights-solution}
\begin{equation}
p_i
\asymp
\exp
\left(
-g_i s
\right)
\qquad
\text{for }
\boldsymbol\alpha_i\notin F.
\label{eq:boundary-weight-s}
\end{equation}
Near $\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}=\boldsymbol\beta$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta-dynamics} becomes
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
=
\mathbf D(\boldsymbol\beta)^{-1}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
-
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
+
O
\left(
\|\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}-\boldsymbol\beta\|^2
\right).
\label{eq:theta-local}
\end{equation}
The normal displacement is generated by the off-face strains and is of order $\exp(-gs)$. Hence, for a nondegenerate codimension-one face,
\begin{equation}
\dot s
\asymp
\exp
\left(
-gs
\right).
\end{equation}
Integration gives
\begin{equation}
s(t)
=
\frac{1}{g}
\log t
+
O(1),
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
\asymp
 t^{-g_i/g}.
\label{eq:boundary-weights}
\end{equation}
In particular, the least-separated off-face strains decay as
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
\asymp
\frac{1}{t}.
\end{equation}

The same depletion appears in the covariance normal to the selected face:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf C(t)
\mathbf n
\asymp
\frac{1}{t}.
\label{eq:boundary-covariance}
\end{equation}
Thus the community can match a boundary supply, but only while progressively losing the resource strategies needed to move away from that face.

\subsection{Supply outside the strategy hull}

Suppose
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\beta
\notin
\mathcal R.
\end{equation}
The community cannot match the supplied resource composition. Instead, the Lyapunov function in Eq.~\eqref{eq:kl} converges to its unique minimizer over the attainable set,
\begin{equation}
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}_*
=
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf x\in\mathcal R}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta
\middle\|
\mathbf x
\right).
\label{eq:kl-projection}
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q_*
=
\boldsymbol\beta
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}_*.
\end{equation}
The first-order condition for the constrained minimum is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q_*^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\mathbf x
-
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}_*
\right)
\leq
0
\qquad
\text{for every }
\mathbf x\in\mathcal R.
\label{eq:kl-support}
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q_*^{\mathsf T}
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}_*
=
1,
\end{equation}
the limiting community lies on the exposed face
\begin{equation}
F_*
=
\left\{
\boldsymbol\alpha_i:
\mathbf q_*^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol\alpha_i
=
1
\right\}.
\end{equation}
For a strain outside this face, define its asymptotic disadvantage
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i
=
1
-
\mathbf q_*^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol\alpha_i
>
0.
\label{eq:outside-gap}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:resource-replicator} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}
\log p_i
\to
-\Delta_i,
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)
=
\exp
\left[
-\Delta_i t
+
o(t)
\right].
\label{eq:outside-weights}
\end{equation}
An unattainable supply composition therefore drives exponential depletion of strategies outside the limiting face.

\subsection{Recovery after a resource switch}

Apply a first resource supply $\boldsymbol\beta^-$ until time $T$, then switch to $\boldsymbol\beta^+$. Suppose strain $i$ was depleted during the first exposure and has positive invasion growth after the switch. Its instantaneous log-frequency growth is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}
\log p_i
=
\boldsymbol\alpha_i^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta^+
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
-1.
\label{eq:post-switch-growth}
\end{equation}
Assume that while $p_i$ grows from $p_i(T)$ to a fixed threshold $\eta$, this rate satisfies
\begin{equation}
0
<
\sigma_-
\leq
\boldsymbol\alpha_i^{\mathsf T}
\left(
\boldsymbol\beta^+
\oslash
\bar{\boldsymbol\alpha}
\right)
-1
\leq
\sigma_+.
\label{eq:growth-bounds}
\end{equation}
If $\tau_i(T)$ is the first time after the switch at which $p_i=\eta$, then
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\sigma_+}
\log
\frac{\eta}{p_i(T)}
\leq
\tau_i(T)
\leq
\frac{1}{\sigma_-}
\log
\frac{\eta}{p_i(T)}.
\label{eq:recovery-bound}
\end{equation}
The depletion law during the first environment therefore directly fixes the later recovery timescale.

For an interior first supply, Eq.~\eqref{eq:interior-frequencies} gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
O(1).
\end{equation}
For a boundary supply, Eq.~\eqref{eq:boundary-weights} gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
\Theta
\left(
\log T
\right).
\label{eq:boundary-recovery}
\end{equation}
For a first supply outside the strategy hull, Eq.~\eqref{eq:outside-weights} gives
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(T)
=
\Theta(T).
\label{eq:outside-recovery}
\end{equation}
Thus the same convex-hull geometry that controls coexistence also controls how ecological history is stored in rare-strain abundances and later appears as a recovery lag.

\section{Discussion}

Resource competition provides a concrete biological realization of the response geometry. The vectors are not generic phenotypes: they are metabolic allocation strategies, the environmental target is the supplied resource composition, and the changing weights are strain frequencies. The convex-hull condition has an established ecological interpretation in models with metabolic trade-offs \cite{posfai2017,taillefumier2017,good2018}, while recent work has emphasized geometric descriptions of coexistence and ecosystem response more broadly \cite{blumenthal2023,goyal2025}. The result derived here concerns a different object: the transient abundance of strategies that are not needed in the current environment but may become important after the environment changes.

The model gives a direct experimental protocol. A microbial community containing strains with measured resource-use strategies can be maintained under a fixed mixture of substitutable carbon sources for different durations $T$, then transferred to a second mixture that favors a strain depleted during the first phase. The theory predicts that the dependence of the recovery lag on $T$ is controlled by whether the first supply lies inside, on the boundary of, or outside the convex hull of the available strategies. Time-dependent resource environments and metabolically adaptive communities are already natural extensions of consumer--resource theory \cite{pacciani2020}; here the emphasis is instead on the memory generated by composition change even when the individual strategies themselves are fixed.

The deterministic model neglects mutation, immigration, and phenotypic switching. Any such process prevents arbitrarily small frequencies and will eventually cut off the asymptotic growth of the recovery lag. A weak replenishment rate therefore introduces a natural crossover: the $\log T$ and $T$ regimes hold while ecological depletion dominates, then saturate once the rare-strain abundance reaches the replenishment floor. This extension does not require a new memory variable; it regularizes the same ecological mechanism.

The central prediction is therefore not that communities possess an abstract adaptability score. It is that prolonged resource selection changes the abundance of alternative metabolic strategies in a geometrically constrained way, and that these abundance tails determine how long the community takes to respond when the resource environment changes.
